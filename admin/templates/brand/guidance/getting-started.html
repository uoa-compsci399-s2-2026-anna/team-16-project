Four things, in this order

The first two are about getting into this panel safely, and nothing else here is safe to do before they are done — which is why the panel made you do most of them before it would show you this page.

  1. Change the password you were issued. Done already: no other screen opens until that password has been replaced, so if you are reading this, yours has been.

    The reason it is forced is that the password you were first given was printed into the log of the job that set this system up, and it is still sitting there. It was never a secret. If somebody else set the account up and handed it to you already working, then they know the password you are using now — change it on Your security.

  2. Enrol an authenticator, and keep a way back in. The first authenticator is done already too — nobody gets in without one. Two things the panel did not make you do:

    Keep the five recovery codes. They were shown once, on the page straight after the QR code, and nothing can show them again. This system sends no email and has no “forgot password” link, so those codes are the only way back in that does not need somebody else. If you did not save them, another administrator can reset your authenticator and start you over; this panel refuses to let an account reset its own, so it has to be somebody else.

    Set up a second device now, before you need it. Your security → Add an authenticator. It takes a minute, both devices keep working, and it is the difference between a lost phone being an inconvenience and being a phone call to whoever else holds an account.

  3. Find the numbers the calculator is answering with. Factor sets. At most one row is ever marked Published, and that is the one every visitor's calculation uses; the rest are drafts or archived.

    Look at Mock data on that published row. It is ticked today. The factors in this system are placeholders — the right shape and the wrong values — and while that tick is on, every result the public sees and every file they export carries a warning saying so, which they cannot dismiss.

    That screen explains the rest, and it is worth reading before changing anything there: what draft, published and archived mean, why a published set's numbers are never edited in place, and what has to happen when the real factors arrive.

  4. Run one calculation, to see the system answer. Try a scenario. Take the values it offers you and press Run.

    Numbers coming back mean the whole chain works: this panel reached the calculation service, which read the published factor set out of the database and worked through its formulas. If it says instead that the service could not be reached, the calculator is down and this panel is not — which is worth knowing on your first day rather than on a day something depends on it.

Then look around

Every box on every form has a line under it saying what it is for, and the screens where the order of things matters carry a block like this one at the top. Between them they are the whole of what this panel writes down, so a screen that looks unexplained probably is not.
